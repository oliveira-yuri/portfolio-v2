When I built a WhatsApp agent to run first-line triage for a law firm's incoming
contacts, the part that took the most time was not the integration. It was
deciding who decides: the language model, or a rule I write myself.

The answer looks obvious today — a model understands natural language better
than any `if` I could write. But the triage did not end at understanding the
message. It ended at sending a person to a specific lawyer. And that changes the
question.

## What happens when the decision is wrong

A classifier that picks the wrong area of law does not produce a slightly worse
answer. It produces a person talking to the wrong professional about a problem
that is usually urgent and almost always uncomfortable to repeat.

That is the test I now apply before handing a decision to the model: **how
expensive is the error, and who pays for it?** If the error is cheap and the
user corrects it in the next message, the model is the right call — it is right
more often, and writing the equivalent rule would be wasted work. If the error
is expensive, lands on someone who is not in the room, and nobody notices it
happened, the explicit rule wins even though it is right less often.

Note that the criterion is not "is the model reliable?". It is the asymmetry of
the cost. A classifier that is right 95% of the time is excellent in one place
and unacceptable in another, and the number alone does not tell you which.

## The second reason: you can point at the mistake

There is a practical difference between a flow that gets things wrong and a flow
that gets things wrong in a way you can explain.

With conditional logic, when a triage comes out wrong I open the execution in
n8n and see exactly which answer produced that classification. The fix is one
line. With the model deciding freely, what I have is a bad output and a prompt
to adjust in the dark — and the same question tomorrow may produce something
else.

In a context where someone may have to account for what the system did, this
stops being an engineering preference. A rule is auditable. A prompt is an
attempt.

## Where the model is still the best part

None of this is an argument against using AI in automation. It is an argument
against using AI for the routing decision.

In that same flow, the model does very well what it does better than any rule:
understanding a message written in a hurry, with typos and no punctuation, and
pulling out of it the data the rule needs — what this is about, whether there
was prior contact, whether the person mentioned a deadline. The model
interprets. The rule decides.

That separation is the design I keep reaching for: extraction and comprehension
by the model, decisions by explicit conditions. It costs more to write than
handing everything to the model, and it pays off in exactly the flows where
somebody depends on the result.

## The summary I wish I had read first

Before putting an LLM in the middle of a flow, three questions are worth
answering:

1. If this decision comes out wrong, who feels it — the user, who fixes it on
   the spot, or a third party, who may never find out?
2. A month from now, can I explain why the system decided that?
3. Is the model here to *interpret* a messy input, or to *choose* what happens
   next?

If the answer to the third is "choose", think twice. Almost every time, what I
actually wanted was the first.
